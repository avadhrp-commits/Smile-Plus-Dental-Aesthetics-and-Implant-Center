Root canal vs. tooth extraction: which is right for you?

An infected or badly damaged tooth usually leaves you with two real options. Here's how to think through the decision honestly.

Medically reviewed by Dr. Yashasvi Patel, Dental Surgeon · Updated September 2026 · 5 min read

A deeply infected or badly damaged tooth usually comes down to one decision: save it with a root canal, or remove it. Both are legitimate, common treatments — the right one depends on the specific tooth, not a blanket rule. Here's how to think it through.

What root canal treatment actually does

Root canal treatment removes infected or inflamed tissue from inside the tooth, then cleans, seals and typically crowns it — keeping your natural tooth in place. Because it preserves your own tooth root, it maintains normal chewing function, keeps neighbouring teeth from shifting, and avoids the bone loss that can follow a missing tooth.

What tooth extraction actually does

Extraction removes the tooth entirely. It's sometimes the more sensible choice when a tooth is fractured below the gumline, when the supporting bone is too damaged to hold it long-term, or when the root canal system is unusually complex or previously failed. It's a shorter procedure on its own — but on its own, it also leaves a gap.

The real trade-off: what happens after

This is the part that's easy to overlook. A root canal ends with your natural tooth still doing its job. An extraction ends with a decision still ahead of you: leave the gap, or replace it with a bridge or a dental implant. Left unreplaced, neighbouring teeth can drift, your bite can shift out of alignment, and the jawbone in that area gradually loses density from lack of use — which can complicate a future implant if you change your mind later.

That's why, cost-for-cost, a root canal and crown often work out comparable to — or less than — an extraction plus a future implant, once you account for the full picture rather than just the first procedure.

When extraction is genuinely the better call

  • The tooth is fractured well below the gumline, with too little structure left to restore.
  • Advanced gum disease has already destroyed most of the supporting bone.
  • A previous root canal on the same tooth has failed and can't be effectively redone.
  • The tooth is a wisdom tooth causing recurring problems with no functional role.

When saving the tooth is usually worth it

  • The infection is limited to the pulp, with healthy structure remaining above and below.
  • The tooth plays an important role in your bite — especially molars and front teeth.
  • You'd prefer to avoid the extra step (and cost) of a future replacement.

How Smile+ helps you decide

An X-ray tells most of this story before treatment even starts — how much healthy structure remains, how the infection has spread, and whether the root canal system is treatable.
